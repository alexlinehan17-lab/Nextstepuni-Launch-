In every direction

What does not change, whichever you pick

These rules fix the faults in your brief. They belong to the system, not to a look.

Navigation
Five places in one bar: Home, Progress, Study, Journey, Launch. On a laptop the same places are the sidebar. The bar shows while you browse and hides while you are in a task.
One way out
Every screen has exactly one leave control, top-left. Back (a chevron) if you tapped into it. Close (an X) if it came up over where you were. Cancel only when there are unsaved changes. Ending a running session is a named choice, never a corner icon.
Safe areas
The top bar, the tab bar and the pinned footer own the space round the Dynamic Island and the home indicator. No screen places anything against the raw edge, so no screen needs its own fix.
Buttons
One shape, three heights (52, 44, 36). Rank is shown by style, never by size. A main button that cannot be used yet says what is missing and takes you there.
Arrows
A chevron means this row opens. An arrow means move forward. A down-chevron means a menu. An up-right arrow means you are leaving the app. Nothing else.
Space
One page margin on every phone screen. One gap between sections, one between items, one inside boxes. Sections never touch.
Dark mode
Four named surfaces and no others, so a section can never be a slightly different black from the page behind it.
Phone containers
Short choices open a menu. Anything longer opens a sheet from the bottom. No centred pop-up cards.
Motion
Short and exact, never bouncy: about a fifth of a second for most things. Nothing moves on things you do many times a minute.

Recommendation

Direction C

It is the only one that keeps both things you have told me you like. A keeps sign-in and loses Home. B keeps Home and loses sign-in and onboarding. C keeps Home almost untouched and keeps the charcoal button, the paper sheet and the one-job-per-screen habit of sign-in and onboarding.

It answers "what counts as a button" with one sentence: a bordered box is something you can press, charcoal is the action, and the orange button means a study session is about to start.

It gives orange a meaning, so the product is recognisable by where the orange is, not by how much of it there is.

What it costs: sign-in's headline changes typeface, and onboarding's capital headlines become sentence case. If you would rather keep those two flows exactly as they are and apply C from the first screen after them, say so: that is a boundary, not a different system.

The standard: ten things sign‑in and onboarding both do

These are the habits the two flows share. None of them is about their layout, so they can be asked of every other screen.

  1. One job per screen, said in words first. A small label saying where you are, one short headline, one line of explanation, then the control. A screen that needs two headlines is two screens.
  2. One main button, always in the same place, and it is charcoal. White label on the left, arrow on the right. Back is quiet text, never a second filled button. Orange is not the button colour.
  3. The frame looks after the edges, so no screen has to. One wrapper sets the side margin and keeps everything clear of the status bar and the home indicator. This rule alone would have prevented your issues 1, 2 and 3.
  4. A short ladder of spaces, used the same way every time. Small between a label and its field, medium between fields, large between blocks. Sections are separated by space or a single hairline, never by a box each.
  5. Hairlines and one sheet, not boxes inside boxes. Thin grey lines, gently rounded controls, and at most one raised surface on a screen. No tinted panels, no gradients, no shadows on ordinary controls.
  6. Colour is rationed, and orange is an event. White, charcoal, one grey and one hairline. Orange appears once or twice on a screen and never tints a card, a tag or an icon tile.
  7. Three sizes of type do the work. A headline, body text and a small label. No bold body text, no in-between sizes, no pill badges.
  8. Everything you can tap is big enough, looks tappable before you touch it, and says its state in words. The chosen tile is unmistakable and its label changes too: Add becomes Added. Fields never make the phone zoom.
  9. Problems are named where they happen, with the way out. A main button that is off has a sentence directly above it saying what is missing. No red boxes, no browser alerts, no buttons that silently do nothing.
  10. Progress is always visible, answers are never lost, and motion and characters serve the task. You can see how far along you are and what you have already given. Movement is short and switches off when the phone asks for less motion. One character per screen, and it shrinks once there is work to do.

Worth knowing

What this work did and did not check

Checked
Every screen was read in the code, and the main screens were photographed running on demo data at iPhone size in light and dark. Counts such as "41 button styles" were produced by scripts, which are saved with the audit.
Not checked
Nothing was looked at on a physical iPhone. Positions near the Dynamic Island are worked out from the code, and they match what you reported. Motion in the mock-ups was built to the numbers, but how it feels in the hand is yours to judge on your phone.
Not changed
The app, its repository and the live site are untouched. Everything here lives in a working folder and on this private page.
Not included
New features, the public landing page, and the wording of the learning modules.
