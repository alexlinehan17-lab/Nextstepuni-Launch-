## Paper shape — three components; Visual Studies is one written paper (printed p.2)

The whole subject is **500 marks** across three components (component table, printed p.2):

| Component | Marks | Weight |
|---|---|---|
| Practical Coursework | 250 | 50% |
| Invigilated Practical Examination | 100 | 20% |
| **Written Examination – Visual Studies** | **150** | **30%** |

The Visual Studies written paper (150) is marked in **three sections** (printed pp.15–24):

- **Section A – Today's world** — **50 marks**. Candidates **answer any five questions from Q1–Q7**,
  **each worth 10 marks** (5 × 10 = 50). (printed p.15)
- **Section B – Europe and the wider world** — **50 marks**. Answer **ONE** question. (printed p.20)
- **Section C – Ireland and its place in the wider world** — **50 marks**. Answer **ONE** question.
  (printed p.20)

So the written total (150) = 50 + 50 + 50, but the **two halves are marked by different machinery**:
Section A uses a compact short-answer descriptor; Sections B & C use a four-strand band rubric.

## The marking grammar is DESCRIPTOR-BAND, not point-allocation (printed p.15)

The scheme states its own method explicitly:

> "This marking scheme uses **descriptor marking** to allocate marks. The mark to be allocated is
> dependent on the mark available for that particular part of the question." (printed p.15)

So there is **no "5 for name / 10 for discussion" itemisation** anywhere in Visual Studies. Instead
each markable unit has a mark ceiling and a **Low / Moderate / High** band, and the examiner reads
the answer against a descriptor to place it in a band. The examiner is told to **re-read each answer
multiple times**, once per criterion:

> "Each time you prepare to award marks associated with a specific part of the marking scheme you
> must read the relevant answer with the criteria for that specific part … In this way, you will
> read each answer a number of times." (printed p.15)

### Section A — "Today's world": short-question descriptor with 4/5/6-mark variants (printed pp.15–19)

Every Section-A question is a **two-part 10-mark question** (part (a) + part (b)), where in 2024 the
parts are worth **4, 5 or 6 marks** each. A single shared descriptor covers all three tariffs, with
band boundaries that scale with the tariff (printed p.16):

| Band | 4-mark part | 5-mark part | 6-mark part |
|---|---|---|---|
| Low | 0–1 | 0–1 | 0–2 |
| Moderate | 2–3 | 2–3 | 3–4 |
| High | 4 | 4–5 | 5–6 |

The descriptor rates the answer on: understanding the requirements of the question; applying
knowledge to "new and unfamiliar situations"; **problem-solving in relation to Visual Studies and
the practical application of art**; analysis/interpretation/evaluation of the given stimulus;
evidence-based arguments; coherence and focus; and "use of visual art terminology and/or sketches"
(printed p.16). Note the shape of the two parts across all seven questions (printed pp.17–19):

- **Part (a)** is almost always a **description/account of the illustrated work "using the given
  headings"** — the paper *names* the lenses (e.g. "composition, perspective, colour"; "use of
  materials, colour, line"; "layout, light, contrast"). This is the higher-tariff part (usually 6).
- **Part (b)** is an **interpretation / opinion / discussion with a justification** ("Reasons for
  the answer given", "Justification for the answer given"). This is the lower-tariff part (usually 4).

So even in the short section, marks reward **covering the named headings** in (a) and **backing an
opinion with evidence** in (b) — not naming facts for their own sake.

### Sections B & C — four band-descriptor strands totalling 50 (printed pp.21–24)

Each 50-mark essay-style question in Sections B and C is marked on **four separate band descriptors**,
each scored Low / Moderate / High, that sum to 50:

| Strand | Marks | Low | Moderate | High | Printed p. |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Coherence and Focus** | 10 | 0–3 | 4–6 | 7–10 | p.21 |
| **Subject Knowledge** | 20 | 0–7 | 8–13 | 14–20 | p.22 |
| **Relevant Examples** | 10 | 0–3 | 4–6 | 7–10 | p.23 |
| **Visual Language (terminology and/or sketches)** | 10 | 0–3 | 4–6 | 7–10 | p.24 |

What each strand rewards (verbatim gist):

- **Coherence and Focus (10):** understanding the demands of the question and building an answer
  *on the question posed*; forming "reasonable, sequential and logical arguments … based on
  evidence"; and articulating a **personal understanding**. (printed p.21)
- **Subject Knowledge (20):** the largest strand — recall/knowledge/understanding of the "section
  of focus", **plus critical thinking to analyse and evaluate** that knowledge, and using it
  "critically or perceptively … to appropriately support their approach to the question". Mere
  recall lands in Low; analysis and evaluation are what lift it to High. (printed p.22)
- **Relevant Examples (10):** the **choice of artists and examples of artworks/artefacts** that are
  relevant/recognised and *understood well enough to justify the response*. Weak, unrecognised or
  irrelevant examples cap this strand. (printed p.23)
- **Visual Language (10):** accurate, relevant **use of visual-art terminology**, **and/or sketches**.
  Sketches are explicitly **conditional** — every descriptor level begins "*If sketches are used*"
  — so a candidate can max this strand on terminology alone, but good sketches are an equal route
  to the marks. (printed p.24)

**Behaviour implication:** a Section B/C answer that is knowledgeable but wanders off the question,
or that recalls facts without analysis, or that names no recognised examples, forfeits a whole
strand's ceiling — the four strands are independent, so a lopsided answer is structurally capped.

## Sketches: now folded into a band, not a ring-fenced fixed award (printed pp.16, 24)

This is the sharpest structural change from the old spec. In 2019, **every** History &
Appreciation question carried a dedicated "Sketches" part worth a fixed 5–10 marks that prose-only
candidates simply surrendered. In Visual Studies, sketches are **optional support** absorbed into
the descriptor:

- General instruction: "Where sketches are included to answer any question, they should be reviewed
  as part of the material on which the mark is based **using the mark allocation for the part of the
  question that the sketch is answering or supporting**." (printed p.15)
- In Sections B & C they can earn up to the full 10-mark **Visual Language** strand, but only "**If
  sketches are used**" — terminology is the alternative route. (printed p.24)

So sketches are no longer a guaranteed slice of marks; they are one of two ways to satisfy the
Visual Language objective and a supporting evidence source everywhere else.

## General examiner instructions worth teaching (printed pp.13, 15, 20)

- **Skilful brevity is protected; padding is not rewarded** — stated twice: "Be careful not to
  penalise skilful brevity, nor to reward unwarranted length." (printed pp.15, 20)
- **Open-example marking:** "Candidates may choose from a wide range of relevant examples and
  content to answer a question. **Relevant content must be credited.**" — the scheme is not a closed
  answer key; any valid, relevant material scores. (printed p.20)
- **Best-answer rule for over-answering:** "If candidates answer more than one question … per
  section then **the question with the highest score is used** to award marks." (printed p.20) —
  contrast History, which *deducts* invalid extra answers; Art simply keeps the best.
- **Examiner must self-educate on unfamiliar works:** "If you are not familiar with the artwork or
  artist in a response it is your responsibility to make yourself familiar … before you mark the
  question. All valid points will be taken into consideration." (printed p.15)
- **Context-dependent detail:** "The detail required in any answer is determined by the context and
  the manner in which the question is asked and by the number of marks assigned." (printed p.15)
- **Terminology must be correct in context:** "Words, expressions or phrases must be correctly used
  in context and not contradicted, and where there is evidence of incorrect use or contradictions
  the marks may not be awarded." (printed p.15)
- **Sparse annotation set:** unlike History's rich annotation machinery, the Visual Studies online
  marking uses effectively two symbols — a **blank Tick** ("Written and/or drawn response seen by
  the examiner") and a **vertical wavy** ("Blank page/section seen by the examiner"). (printed p.13)
- **The Irish Bonus** applies: 10% of marks obtained for candidates answering entirely through Irish
  who score under 75% (a scaled table above 75%), decimals always rounded down. (printed pp.13–14)
- Standard SEC front matter (printed p.1): the scheme is "not intended to be standalone documents …
  an essential resource for examiners who receive training in the correct interpretation and
  application of the scheme" — the printed descriptors are a trained-examiner skeleton.

## How this differs from the old spec (History & Appreciation, 2019)

| | **2019 History & Appreciation** (old spec) | **2024 Visual Studies** (revised spec) |
|---|---|---|
| Written paper name | History and Appreciation of Art | Visual Studies |
| Total | 150 marks (3 × 50, one Q per section) | 150 marks (Section A 50 + B 50 + C 50) |
| Marking grammar | **Point-allocation** — fixed mark per lettered part | **Descriptor bands** — Low/Moderate/High per strand |
| Naming | "5 for name" as a separate itemised award | No naming tariff; folded into descriptors |
| Discussion | Steered by *named lenses* inside each part | Section B/C: four named strands (Coherence, Subject Knowledge, Relevant Examples, Visual Language) |
| Sketches | **Ring-fenced 5–10 marks in every question** (prose-only forfeits) | **Optional** — one route to the 10-mark Visual Language strand; "if sketches are used" |
| Section A | (n/a — three 50-mark sections) | Compact 2-part 10-mark short questions, 4/5/6-mark descriptor |
| Over-answering | (choose one per section) | **Best-scoring answer counts** |

The single biggest teaching consequence: on the **new** paper a candidate is rewarded for
**analysis, coherence, recognised examples and correct terminology as separable objectives**, and
sketching is no longer a guaranteed pot of marks — whereas on the **old** paper marks were a sum of
fixed, itemised slices (name + description + a compulsory sketch) with no holistic band.
