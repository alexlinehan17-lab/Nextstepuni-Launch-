## Does German match French's two-axis written-production system? — MOSTLY YES, with real differences

German written production, like French, is scored on **two components that are marked separately and summed**:

- **Content** (≈ French "Communication") and **Expression** (≈ French "Language").
- Schriftliche Produktion (the big 160-word task): **Content = 25 + Expression = 25** (p.[16], [20]) — equal weighting, exactly like French's 30+30 / 20+20.
- Äußerung zum Thema (the 25-mark opinion piece): **Content = 13 + Expression = 12** (p.[13], [15]) — content weighted *slightly* heavier, unlike French's strict equality.

So the **headline principle is the same as French**: content and language are assessed independently, one can be strong while the other is weak. But the **mechanics differ in four structural ways** French does not share:

1. **Content is a points checklist, not a holistic band.** French scores Communication holistically on a 3-band TOP/MIDDLE/BOTTOM descriptor grid. German instead atomises Content into fixed lettered sub-tasks (A, B, C, D, E, F), each worth a stated number of marks awarded point-by-point (e.g. "Satz 1: 1 mark", p.[13]–[18]), **plus explicit discretionary marks** (2 in Äußerung, 5 in Schriftliche Produktion) for "additional relevant elaboration or comment" (p.[13], [16]). French has no discretionary-mark mechanism.
2. **Expression is a FOUR-band grid, not French's three bands** (p.[15], [20]).
3. **The "Lower E" scale-switch** — German's most distinctive feature (see below). French has no equivalent; French just picks a band on the full scale.
4. **Half marks are the universal penalty currency** in German comprehension (wrong language, unmanipulated quotation, extraneous material all → half marks), where French uses a "−1/−2 excess" + "max −1 per segment" ladder.

## THE MARKING GRAMMAR — written production (the core of the tool)

### The two written-production tasks & their splits

| Task | Total | Content | Expression | Discretionary | Where |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Äußerung zum Thema** (opinion/reaction, ≈60+ words) — choice (a) or (b) | 25 | 13 | 12 | 2 (within Content) | p.[13]–[15] |
| **Schriftliche Produktion** (letter OR photo-stimulus, ~160 words) — choice (a) or (b) | 50 | 25 | 25 | 5 (within Content) | p.[16]–[20] |

- **Answer any four of five (letter) / five of six (photo) bullet points; "mark all and pick the best" if more attempted** (p.[16], [17]). Over-answering is not penalised — the best sub-answers are selected.
- Letter option requires **an opening and a closing**: "Op. (2)" — suitable opening (1) + elaboration/reference to the letter (1); "Cl. (2)" — suitable transition to ending (1) + appropriate closing formula (1) (p.[16]). Missing the salutation/sign-off structure loses marks a photo-response candidate would not lose.

### The Expression grid — four bands (verbatim descriptors, p.[20] for Schriftliche Produktion; p.[15] for Äußerung)

Expression is located by taking "a global view of the language use" and placing the script in the best-fitting category (p.[20]). The four bands, worst → best, for Schriftliche Produktion (full scale 0–25):

| Band | Full-scale range | Descriptor (verbatim) |
|---|---|---|
| Lowest | 0 – 6 | "Vocabulary very inadequate, possibly with English words and interference from English syntax; word order, esp. verb position, very frequently wrong; verb forms/tense generally incorrect. Many spelling mistakes, serious grammar errors: cases generally incorrect; few correct agreements." |
| Low | 7 – 14 | "Vocabulary use limited – at times inadequate and inappropriate – with some interference from English; word order mistakes evident. Verb forms/tense frequently incorrect. Frequent spelling mistakes. Occasional serious grammar errors: Cases, agreements, incorrect endings." |
| Good | 15 – 19 | "Vocabulary use quite good – generally adequate and appropriate, with perhaps some German idiom. Only occasional word order mistakes. Few errors in verb forms/tenses. Not too many spelling mistakes. Few serious/frequent minor grammar errors: Cases, agreements, endings correct more often than not." |
| Best | 20 – 25 | "Vocabulary use good – rich, idiomatic and appropriate. Few word order mistakes. Good verb forms/tense formation. Spelling mistakes rare, grammar generally correct: Good level of accuracy in cases, verb endings, agreements…" |

The five named Expression failure points are German-specific: **vocabulary / English interference**, **word order (esp. verb position)**, **verb forms & tense**, **spelling**, and **cases / agreements / endings**. (Note "word order, esp. verb position" and "cases" reflect German's verb-second and four-case grammar — the French grid names "verbs, agreement, spelling" instead.)

### The "Lower E" scale-switch — the distinctive German rule

If a script is **weak on Content OR too short**, Expression is marked on a **reduced scale**, not the full one:

- **Schriftliche Produktion:** "If the content mark is 12 or less, **or the answer is too short (less than 100 words)**, mark expression out of 18" (not 25) — annotate `Lower E` (p.[20]).
- **Äußerung zum Thema:** "If the content mark is 8 or less, **or the answer is too short (less than 60 words)**, mark expression out of 7" (not 12) — annotate `Lower E` (p.[15]).

Each band therefore has two mark ranges — a full-scale range and a Lower-E range (e.g. the top band is 20–25 full / 14–18 Lower E; p.[20]). **Practical consequence for the tool:** a candidate who writes too little or whose content is thin has their language mark structurally capped — even flawless German cannot score full Expression if the piece is under length or under-developed. French has no such length/content gate on the Language axis.

### Reasonable Accommodations (spelling/grammar waiver) — inline, not an appendix

For candidates granted a spelling/grammar waiver, Expression "will be based on the vocabulary, tense formation and word order elements only i.e. the descriptors in italics above and **not on spelling and spelling-related grammar**" (p.[15], [20]). (The scheme prints the spelling-related clauses in italics so examiners know which to ignore.) This mirrors French's Modified Marking Scheme intent (accents/spelling not penalised).

## THE MARKING GRAMMAR — reading comprehension (Leseverständnis, 2 × 60 marks)

Two reading texts, **Text I** and **Text II**, each 60 marks, each with a mix of German-answer and English-answer questions.

### The universal comprehension rules (p.[3], [5], [10])
- **"Evidence needed that candidates have understood the text"** (p.[5], [10]) — the operative principle.
- **Quotation / lifting vs manipulation:** "Quotation without manipulation where manipulation required / containing extraneous material: **half marks**. Full marks for manipulated parts." (p.[5], [10]). Where the question demands the candidate transform the text (change person, tense, etc.), a raw lift is **halved**, not zeroed — but not full.
- **Answers in the language not specified = half marks** (p.[3], [5], [10]; annotation on p.[4]). This is the single most-cited penalty in the scheme.
- **Rounded brackets ( ) = non-essential** material ("not considered to be essential in order to gain full marks"); **underlined = essential** ("essential in order to gain the mark"); **forward slash / = synonym/alternative** ("cannot therefore be taken as different answers") (p.[3]).
- **More than one attempt: "accept the first answer only"** (p.[3]) — hedging with a second answer is ignored; only answer #1 counts.
- **"Cancelled answers should be considered where no other answer has been given"** (p.[3]) — a struck-out answer is only re-read if nothing else is offered.

### Answer-language is prescribed per question (a mix, like French's "opinion in English")
- Some sub-questions must be answered **in German** — flagged "Schreiben Sie … in der Ich-Form" / "Schreiben Sie drei kurze Sätze" (e.g. Text I Q1, p.[5]; Text II Q1, p.[10]).
- Others carry an explicit instruction: **"Answer Question 2 and Question 3 in English"** (p.[11]). Answering these in German → half marks.
- **Wrong-language answers are always half marks, never zero** (p.[3]–[5], [10]).

### Tense / form is often mark-critical (German-specific rigour)
- **Wrong tense zeroes the point** where the question fixes a tense: "(NB: Present Tense = 0)" for a question about past hobbies (Text I Q1a, p.[5]); conversely "Present Tense needed", "Past Tense needed" elsewhere (p.[5], [10]–[12]).
- **"Ich-Form" (first person) required** where specified (p.[5], [10]).
- **"Penalise incorrect verb ending once"** — a repeated verb-ending slip is docked only a single time (p.[5], [10]).
- **Referential accuracy:** "Reference to Feli as 'he' = 0. Penalise once only" (Text I Q3b, p.[7]) — a content point built on a mis-gendered/mis-identified referent scores zero, but only once.

## THE MARKING GRAMMAR — Angewandte Grammatik (Applied Grammar, 25 marks) — NOT present in French

A discrete, purely grammatical manipulation task attached to Text I (p.[9]), **choice of two**:
- **Option 1 — Verbs (5 × 5 marks):** each item scores **verb stem/lexis (2) + correct tense (2) + correct number/agreement (1)**. E.g. "versuchen (2), Perfekt (2), Singular (1)". Getting the verb right but the tense wrong forfeits 2 of 5.
- **Option 2 — Adjective endings (5 × 5 marks):** supply the missing German adjective ending, e.g. "groß-**es** (5)". All-or-nothing per ending.

This atomised accuracy task has **no analogue in the French scheme** and reflects German's inflectional grammar (case-driven adjective endings, tense/number verb morphology). For the tool, it is a clean example of "pure form, marked mechanically" — the opposite pole from the holistic Expression grid.

## THE MARKING GRAMMAR — Listening/Aural (80 marks)

Four parts: Interview (25), Telephone call (16), Conversation (18), News (21) (p.[21]–[28]).

- **Answer-language penalty flips by section:**
  - Interview / most sections: expected in **English** — "Where answers are in German, award half marks" (p.[21]).
  - Telephone-call note-taking (Part 2 Q1): the candidate must "**Write down in German** the key information … Write key phrases, not full sentences" — here **German is required**, and "Where answers are in English/Irish, award half marks" (p.[23]). So the same half-marks penalty applies, but the *expected* language inverts within the aural test.
- **"Language-spotting" sub-task:** Part 2 Q2 asks for "three examples of the language used (= expressions and phrases) which show that the caller is happy" — "**no marks for tone of voice / intonation without reference to specific expressions and phrases**"; accept "accurate/exact phrase or accurate paraphrase … in translation" (p.[24]). Marks attach to cited lexis, not to a vague impression.
- **"All or nothing" items:** e.g. the contact number "0171 63 72 9 1 0" (2 marks, all-or-nothing) and the age range "16 to 80" (all-or-nothing) (p.[23], [27]).
- **Precise-detail discrimination — near-miss vocabulary scores 0:** "Plural = 0" / "Plurals needed" (p.[27]); "overcast = 0" (vs "cloudy", p.[28]); "Controls = 0" (vs "monitors/checks", p.[12] — same rigour in reading); "Are healthier = 0" (vs "eat more healthily", p.[27]); "Laugh = 0" (vs "smile", p.[7]). The scheme repeatedly zeroes an *almost-right* word.

## General examiner instructions & annotations (p.[3]–[4])

The annotation palette (examiner shorthand, p.[4]):
- **Irrelevant material** (bracket off with square brackets `[ … ]`, p.[19]) · **Good point of language** · **Half-mark-penalty marker** — "for answers in language not specified / extraneous material / quotation without manipulation where manipulation required" · **Lower Expression** (`Lower E`) · **error marker** — "Grammar mistakes, tenses, inaccurate vocabulary use, spelling mistakes, omissions" · **0 marks** · numeric marks awarded · **Content marks** (`Con`) for parts a, b, c… · **Blank page / material seen** · **Word order mistake** (its own dedicated annotation) · `Op1`/`Cl1` opening/closing sentence · `Dis1` discretionary mark · `MMS` Modified Marking Scheme.
- **Global rule (p.[3]):** "Where the candidate answers a question more than once, accept the first answer only. Where answers are in the language other than specified: award half marks."
- **Content and Expression "to be marked consecutively"** (p.[19]) — the examiner completes the content pass, then the expression pass, keeping the two axes procedurally separate (the operational counterpart of the two-component principle).

## Quotable lines (with page refs)

1. **The two-component split (p.[16], [20]):** written production is scored `Content = 25 marks / Expression = 25 marks` (Schriftliche Produktion) and `Content = 13 / Expression = 12` (Äußerung) — content and language marked separately, and (for the big task) equally.
2. **The Lower-E length/content gate (p.[20]):**
   > "If the content mark is 12 or less, or the answer is too short (less than 100 words), mark expression out of 18."
3. **The half-marks currency for wrong language (p.[3]):**
   > "Where answers are in the language other than specified: award half marks."
4. **Quotation without manipulation is halved, not zeroed (p.[5], [10]):**
   > "Quotation without manipulation where manipulation required / containing extraneous material: half marks. Full marks for manipulated parts."
5. **The bottom Expression band names English interference as the failure mode (p.[20]):**
   > "Vocabulary very inadequate, possibly with English words and interference from English syntax; word order, esp. verb position, very frequently wrong; verb forms/tense generally incorrect."
6. **Near-right is wrong — the zero-for-imprecision rule (p.[27], [28], [7]):**
   > "overcast = 0" · "Are healthier = 0" · "Reference to Feli as 'he' = 0. Penalise once only" — the scheme repeatedly zeroes an almost-correct word.
7. **Over-answering is safe; the best sub-answers are kept (p.[16]):**
   > "Where a candidate has answered more than four, mark all and pick the best four for final mark."

## Documented candidate-behaviour findings (for authored "scripts")

Each is a marking rule that predicts a specific way candidates gain/lose marks — sourced to this scheme:

1. **"Write more, but hit length" — the Lower-E trap.** A candidate who writes beautiful but short German (under 100 words in Schriftliche Produktion / under 60 in Äußerung) has Expression capped on the reduced 0–18 / 0–7 scale — flawless language *cannot* score full marks below length. (Source: marking scheme p.[15], [20].)
2. **"Don't just copy the text back."** Where a comprehension question requires manipulation, lifting the sentence verbatim earns **half marks, not zero and not full** — the candidate must transform person/tense. Copying is a partial-credit habit that quietly caps the reading score. (p.[5], [10].)
3. **"Answer in the language the question asks for."** German-answer and English-answer questions are mixed within each reading text ("Answer Question 2 and Question 3 in English", p.[11]; "in der Ich-Form" elsewhere). Using the wrong language → automatic half marks on that point. (p.[3], [11].)
4. **"Get the tense the question fixes."** Tense is frequently mark-critical: a past-events question is scored "Present Tense = 0" (p.[5]); other points say "Past Tense needed". Right content in the wrong tense can score nothing. (p.[5], [10]–[12].)
5. **"Precision beats approximation."** The scheme repeatedly zeroes a near-synonym: `overcast` for "cloudy", `Are healthier` for "eat more healthily", singular where a plural is required. Vague or approximate vocabulary forfeits the point even when the gist is right. (p.[7], [12], [27], [28].)
6. **"Don't hedge with a second answer."** "Accept the first answer only" — offering two answers to bank one right does not work; only answer #1 is marked. (p.[3].)
